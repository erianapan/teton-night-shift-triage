Problem framing

What I set out to answer before drawing anything, the people it is for, and the eight statements the prototype was built against.

If a night assistant is alone on a floor of twenty residents at 03:00, what should the phone in her pocket be allowed to ask of her?

Self-set brief. Teton has solved detection to a high standard and published the numbers. The open question is not what the building can see, it is what a tired person should be asked to do about it.

Who is awake at 03:00

Night assistantprimary

Holding something, mid-task, half the floor away. Needs one answer: do I stand up, and which door.

The other assistantsecondary

In another corridor. Needs to know which rooms are already covered, and to be reachable without a hunt for a number.

The residentaffected

Either asking for a person, or not able to. Should be reached fast, and not woken by someone checking a number.

Night coordinatorsecondary

Off the floor. Called rarely, and when called should already know the room and the resident.

Head of carenext morning

Needs patterns, response times and what to raise at handover. None of it during the night.

Familyout of scope here

Named because decisions about alert thresholds eventually reach them, not because this screen serves them.

The problem, stated

The product measures itself in alerts delivered. The person measures the night in walks taken. Nothing on the screen converts one into the other, so the work of deciding is left with the most interrupted person in the building.

One question, three answers

Principles I held the design to

  1. Name things for the action, never for the alarm

    If a label needs translating into a decision, it has spent attention and bought nothing.

  2. Spend attention per decision, not per event

    Two hundred events is not two hundred decisions. The screen should show the decisions.

  3. Where before what before who

    You navigate by room number. The name matters once you are walking, not before.

  4. If the building can see it, do not ask the person to confirm it

    Taps exist because systems are blind. This one is not.

  5. Analysis belongs to the morning

    Charts and trends are real value, delivered to the wrong person at the wrong hour.

The statements the screen was built against

US-1 · night assistant

When an alert arrives, I need to know within two seconds whether to stand up, because I am usually holding something or already with a resident.

The hard part

Severity words describe the alert, not the response. "Critical" still needs translating by the person with the least capacity to translate.

Decided

Three sections named for the action: Go now, On your 04:00 round, Later. Severity labels deleted entirely.

US-2 · night assistant

When I decide to go, I need the room number before anything else, because that is what I navigate by in a dark corridor.

The hard part

Resident name feels like the humane thing to lead with, and it is the wrong thing to lead with. It does not tell you which door.

Decided

Card order is room, reason, person. Room number set largest on the card.

US-3 · night assistant

I need to know why this one is urgent for this resident, because the same bed exit means different things for different people.

The hard part

The reason has to be visible without being read. A sentence explaining confidence scores is data, and data is not what the moment is for.

Decided

A short pill beside the name, High fall risk, carrying the per-resident risk the platform already calculates. The explanation moved out of the card.

US-4 · night assistant

I should not have to confirm I am going, because at 03:00 with a possible fall on screen I will not stop to tap a button.

The hard part

Acknowledgement is how every alerting product knows somebody responded. Remove the tap and you appear to lose the signal.

Decided

The room reports the arrival instead. Entering marks the alert attended, with nothing tapped. "I am going" survives, demoted, for the walk.

US-5 · night assistant

When something is beyond me, I need help in one tap, and I should not be reading a phone number in a corridor.

The hard part

Escalation is three different jobs: who is on the floor, hand this one over, go above the floor. Putting all three on every card made none of them usable.

Decided

Colleague alert sticky at the top. One escalation ladder at thumb height, which turns yellow once the colleague is already coming.

US-6 · the other assistant

I need to know a room is already covered, so we do not both walk to it while a second resident waits.

The hard part

Attendance has been a personal act recorded on one phone. It needs to be a fact about the building, held in one place.

Decided

Shared attendance state, so presence suppresses the alert on the other phone and the wall screen at the same moment. Marked as a proposal, since Teton has not published it.

US-7 · night assistant

On my round I want to walk the corridor once, but see the person most likely to need me first.

The hard part

Those two orders disagree. Sort by risk and you zig-zag the floor. Sort by corridor and a high-risk resident waits behind routine checks.

Decided

Risk tier first, corridor order within each tier. One walk, and the people who matter most are at the top of it.

US-8 · head of care

I need the night's patterns for handover, and the person on the floor needs them not to be in the way.

The hard part

The hourly chart is the most attractive thing to build and the least useful thing at 03:00. It was the first thing on screen in version one.

Decided

Folded shut at the bottom, labelled for the morning meeting. Still one tap away for whoever wants it.

Scope

Designed here

  • The phone screen a floor assistant carries at night
  • Triage, escalation and arrival for one floor, one shift
  • How attendance is shared between the two staff on duty

Deliberately not designed

  • The wall screen and the desktop, named only where this screen must agree with them
  • Onboarding, settings, and alert configuration
  • Anything a family member sees
  • Day shift, where staffing and interruption patterns are different enough to need their own research

What would show this framing is wrong

  • If the phone is not actually the primary night surface, and the wall screen carries more of the work than assumed, the whole hierarchy moves.
  • If alerts arriving while staff are mid-task are simply lost rather than deferred, then "Go now" has a hole in it that no sorting fixes.
  • If staff have already stopped believing a particular alert type, the ranking is cosmetic and the real work is rebuilding trust.
  • If nobody opens the shift review, the handover needs a different artefact entirely, not a folded chart.
